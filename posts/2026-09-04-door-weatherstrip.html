If you can see parking-lot light under the front door, you are paying to heat the porch.

Exterior doors fail at the gasket long before the slab rots. The factory kerf-in vinyl dries out, the corner pads fall off, the sweep chews itself on a proud threshold, and nobody notices until the hallway is a wind tunnel in January. Foam tape from the grocery store lasts one season and turns into black crumbs. Do the job with the right profile once.

This is a weather job, not a new door. If the slab is delaminating or the frame is banana-shaped, stop and replace the unit. Weatherstrip will not straighten a hinge that let the door drop half an inch.

Find the leaks before you buy foam

Daylight is honest. Close the door from inside at noon and look at the head, latch jamb, hinge jamb, and sill. A credit card should drag, not fall through. On a dark evening, have someone wave a flashlight around the outside while you watch the crack. A wet hand feels draft better than a $20 “detector.”

Check the latch. If you have to lift the knob to make the bolt hit, the hinges sagged. Tighten the long screws in the top hinge into the jack stud — replace the 3/4-inch factory screws with 2-1/2 or 3-inch #9s. A door that does not meet the stop will eat every gasket you install.

Kerf weatherstrip beats peel-and-stick

Most prehung exterior doors have a slot (kerf) in the jamb stop. The replacement is a vinyl or silicone bulb with a barb that pushes into that slot. Pull the old strip starting at a corner. Measure the three legs separately; miter or overlap the top piece so the corners are not a hole. Silicone bulb stays soft longer than cheap vinyl. Buy the profile that matches the kerf width, not whatever was on the end cap at the box store.

No kerf? Use slotted metal or vinyl J-channel nailed to the jamb stop with 1-inch brads, or a silicone compression strip screwed on. Do not stack three layers of adhesive foam and call it engineered. Foam is for a temporary winter and for the odd gap behind a deadbolt plate.

The sweep is the part people skip

A worn sweep is a 1/2-inch rectangular hole the width of the door. Screw-on aluminum carriers with a vinyl or silicone insert are serviceable. Cut the carrier a hair under the door width so it does not scrape the jamb. Screw into the slab, not into a foam core with 1/2-inch screws that spin. On a steel door, self-drilling screws; pre-drill if you like your drill bits.

Set the height so the insert kisses the threshold across the whole width. Too hard and the door will not latch. Too light and water walks under it. If the threshold is U-shaped and missing its vinyl cap, replace the cap — that groove is the actual seal. A sweep dragging on bare aluminum will howl and die.

Door sweeps that staple onto the interior face look ugly and catch a rug. Fine for a shop. On a front door, put the carrier on the exterior bottom edge if the door design allows, or use a U-shaped wrap-around sweep made for that slab thickness.

Threshold and corner pads

Adjustable thresholds have screws along the top. Raise the center if you see light there; do not crank one end until the door binds. A dab of silicone under a loose threshold slows the next leak. Corner seal pads — the little bumper at the bottom of each jamb — are not decoration. They close the notch where jamb weatherstrip and sweep fail to meet. Stick or screw new ones. The old ones are why the rug is always damp in that corner.

Do not caulk the door shut

Caulk the exterior brickmold to the siding. Do not caulk the movable slab to the jamb. The door has to open. If the frame-to-rough-opening gap was never foamed, that is a low-expansion foam job from inside after you pull casing, not a tube of painter’s caulk smeared on the stop.

Lock the deadbolt and check operation after the strip is in. A fat bulb can push the latch out of alignment. Plane nothing until the hinges and strike are right.

A Saturday sequence

  1. Tighten hinges into framing. Confirm the latch hits without a hip-check.
  2. Pull old kerf strip. Vacuum the slot. Press in new bulb, corners tight.
  3. Replace corner pads.
  4. Swap or trim the sweep. Adjust threshold so the insert compresses evenly.
  5. Dark-room flashlight test. If a line of light remains at the head, the stop or the slab is twisted — that is plane or shim work, not more foam.

Weatherstrip is cheap. Heated air is not. If the dog still finds a draft after this, the problem is the frame or the attic, not the $12 bulb you just seated.
