If water can wick behind the tub surround, you are farming rot under the tile. Cut the old bead and do it once.

Kitchen-and-bath silicone exists because showers move and tile does not. The factory bead on a 15-year-old tub is cracked, pulled off the enamel, or growing a science project in the inside corner. Water does not need a waterfall. Capillary action through a hairline gap will wet the backer and the joists. You smell it months before the ceiling downstairs stains.

Paintable latex “kitchen caulk” in a shower is a hobby. It shrinks, it mildews, and it peels in sheets. Use 100 % silicone rated for kitchen and bath, mildew-resistant. Clear or white. Not construction adhesive. Not the leftover tube from the exterior trim.

Rip the old stuff out like you mean it

A sharp utility blade, a plastic caulk tool, and patience. Score both edges of the bead. Pull. If it smears into a gummy rope, you had latex. If it comes off in rubbery strips, you had silicone. Get the residue off the enamel and the tile. Mineral spirits or a dedicated silicone remover helps. Do not grind the glaze off the tub with a sanding sponge because you were annoyed.

The joint must be clean and dry. Run the fan. Wait. If the gap is wet, you are sealing a swamp. Wipe with isopropyl and let it flash off. Mask both sides of the joint with painter’s tape, leaving a 6–8 mm gap you can actually tool. Tape is not cheating. Tape is how you avoid a 2-inch smear you then “smooth” with a finger full of dish soap for twenty minutes.

Where the bead belongs

Tub to wall. Tub to tile surround. Inside vertical corners of a surround if they were never sealed. The joint at the drain flange is a gasket job, not a caulk job — if that leaks, you pull the overflow plate and deal with the drain, you do not glob silicone into the overflow like frosting.

Leave a tiny gap at the lowest point of a tub-to-tile joint if the manufacturer says so, so the surround can drain if water gets behind it. Most plastic surrounds want that. Most tiled walls do not. Read the sheet that came with the unit instead of copying a forum post from 2009.

Lay a bead you can tool in one pass

Cut the nozzle at 45 degrees, small opening. You can always cut more. Puncture the foil. Push the gun, do not drag a starved line. A continuous triangle that wets both faces is the goal. Start in a corner and keep moving. If you stop, you get a lump.

Tool immediately. A dedicated plastic spoon-shaped tool, or a gloved finger dipped in a drop of soapy water, not a soup of water that runs down the tile and under the tape. One pass. Pull the tape while the silicone is still wet, pulling away from the joint at a low angle. If you wait until it skins, the tape takes half the bead with it and you swear in two languages.

Cure time is not a suggestion

Silicone skins in minutes and cures in hours to a day depending on humidity and bead size. Do not shower for 24 hours. Steam is water. If the household cannot skip a day, use a different bathroom or accept that you just washed the uncured bead down the overflow.

A thick gloppy fillet takes longer to cure in the middle and stays tacky. A too-thin smear tears on the first expansion of the tub when someone fills it with hot water. The tub flexes. The wall does not. That is the entire reason this joint exists.

Mold is a symptom

Black dots on the surface of mildew-resistant silicone after a year usually mean the bathroom never dries. Run the fan 20 minutes after the shower. Squeegee the corners. If the bead is soft, brown underneath, or the tile sounds hollow, you have water behind the wall and caulk will not save the studs. Cut a hole, look, replace wet board. Sealing over rot is how you buy a bigger project on a credit card.

Do this, then stop touching it

  1. Cut and clean until you see bare enamel and bare tile.
  2. Dry the joint. Tape the edges.
  3. 100 % silicone, small nozzle, one continuous bead.
  4. Tool once. Pull tape. Leave it alone for a day.
  5. If it fails in six months, the room is wet or you used the wrong tube — not “bad luck.”

A shower joint is a gasket you can replace for the price of a tube and an evening. The subfloor is not. Recaulk when the bead cracks, not when the plaster downstairs blooms.
