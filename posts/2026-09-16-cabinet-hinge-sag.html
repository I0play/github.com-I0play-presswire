That door droops because the cup screws are spinning in dust. Tighten the cams before you buy new boxes.

The upper cabinet door no longer meets the neighbor. You slam it. It still hangs low on the latch side. You assume the slab is banana-shaped and start shopping doors. Stop. Most kitchen doors are still flat. The European hinge walked. Particle board does not hold a 5 mm screw forever, and the three adjustment screws on the hinge arm exist because factories expect you to tune this once a year.

A sagging door is a geometry problem. Fix the mount, then use the screws that actually move the plate. Replacing the whole cabinet because a hinge cup is loose is how you spend a weekend for a two-dollar fastener.

Read the hinge before you yank it

Typical overlay kitchen hinge: a cup in the door, an arm to a mounting plate on the box. Two screws in the cup. Two in the plate. Three adjustments on the arm — in and out for overlay, side to side for the gap, and a cam or slot for height. They are not mystery knobs. One turn is a millimeter, not a personality change.

If the door dropped but the gap at the hinge side is tight and the latch side is low, the plate slid down or the cup screws let the door rotate. If every door in the run droops the same way, the box itself sagged off the wall. That is a different job: find the rail screws into studs. Do not chase hinge cams on a cabinet that is falling.

Screws that spin are done

Back the cup screws out. If the hole is oval and the threads are flour, a longer screw into nothing still fails. Options that work: a plastic hinge-repair insert sized for the cup hole, a hardwood dowel glued in and redrilled, or a proper repair bushing. Toothpicks and glue are a temporary for a single stripped wood screw in solid stock. In particle board they last until the next humid week.

The plate on the cabinet wall likes the same treatment. If the plate can rock, the door will never stay aligned. Pull it, fill or insert, drive new screws until the plate does not wiggle when you tug the arm.

Adjust in the right order

Height first so the top edges line up across the run. Then side-to-side so the reveal between doors is even, about 2 to 3 mm on most kitchens. Then overlay depth so the door sits flush with the face, not proud and not sucked into the box. Do one door. Close it. Look from the front, not from your hip.

Soft-close pistons that slam or refuse to pull shut are often just dirty or set too aggressive. Wipe the arm. Do not oil the cup like a gate hinge. Oil on a kitchen door collects flour and becomes paste. If the damper is dead, replace the hinge as a pair on that door so the close rate matches.

When the door really is the problem

A solid-wood door can cup. Check it on a flat table. A 3 mm banana does not get fixed by a cam. Swap the slab or live with a reveal that you tune to the warp. A cheap foil-wrapped particle door that chipped at the cup needs a new door, not more torque. Stripped cup holes in a crumbling slab are the same verdict.

Handles that sit low because you hung the door once and never came back are not a hinge fault. Measure handle height from the finished floor or from the counter line and copy it. Eyeballing six doors is how you get a staircase of pulls.

Prevent the next droop

Do not hang a towel on a door that is already working the screws. Do not use the door as a step stool. Check the run when seasons change. Wood and board move. Two minutes with a screwdriver beats a call to a cabinet shop that will sell you a new kitchen.

A kitchen checklist

  1. Confirm the box is tight to the wall. Loose rail screws first.
  2. If a hinge screw spins, insert or dowel. Do not keep tightening dust.
  3. Height, then side gap, then overlay. One door at a time.
  4. Replace dead soft-close hinges in pairs on that door.
  5. No towels on the handle, no standing on the door. Recheck after a humid week.

A crooked door is usually three screws and a plate that moved. Fix the holes, then use the cams like they were designed. The slab was probably fine the whole time.
