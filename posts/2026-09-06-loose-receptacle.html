If the outlet rocks when you plug in a vacuum, the yoke is loose and the next spark is not a personality. Fix the box.

You shove a two-prong into the living-room receptacle and the whole device tilts. The plate hides it. The plug hangs by one blade. That is not “old house charm.” That is a hot and a neutral that move every time someone uses the lamp. Moving contacts heat. Heat loosens the screw more. Then you get the brown mark on the plate and a story for the insurance adjuster.

A duplex receptacle is held by two 6-32 screws through the yoke into the ears of the box. Drywall is not a box. If those screws are one turn from falling out, or the box itself is floating in a cutout the size of a postcard, the device will walk. You do not need a new circuit. You need the device flush, the wires landed on the side screws, and the box actually attached to something.

Kill the breaker. Not the plate.

Test the receptacle with a plug-in tester or a meter before you believe the marker on the panel. Panels lie. Pull the plate. Look at the yoke. If the device sits proud of the wall because someone stacked drywall and never used the plaster ears, the plug will rock even with tight screws. Break off the plaster ears only when the box is already flush. If the wall grew in front of the box, you need a box extender, not a longer screw into air.

Backstab holes on the rear of cheap devices are how houses get intermittent lamps. The spring inside fatigues. Pull the wire, straighten it, land it under the side screw clockwise so the loop tightens as you turn. 12 AWG on 20 A circuits, 14 AWG on 15 A. Mixing those because “the spool was closer” is how you get a 15 A device feeding a heater on a 20 A breaker. Match the device rating to the breaker.

The box has to exist

Old work plastic boxes are fine when the wings bite the drywall and the drywall is not crumbly. If the gypsum is dust, the wings spin and the box becomes a pendant. Replace the box or add a brace. Metal boxes need the ground bonded to the box, not just to the device. A floating metal box is a surprise when your knuckle finds it.

Box fill is not a suggestion. Count the wires, the device, the grounds. If you are stuffing four 12 AWG cables plus a GFCI into a pancake box, stop. Move to a deeper box. Pinched insulation at the clamp is how a ground becomes a hot on a Tuesday.

The plug should not feel like a suggestion

If a plug falls out under its own weight, the contacts in the receptacle are sprung. Replace the device. Do not pinch the slots with pliers. Do not wrap the blades with foil. Buy a spec-grade duplex if the outlet sees a vacuum every week. The extra dollars buy contacts that still grip after a thousand insertions.

USB receptacles that died after two years were cheap boards in a hot box. If you want USB, put a charger on the desk and keep the wall device a dumb pair of slots. Heat plus a plastic yoke is how the mounting ears warp and the whole thing starts the wobble again.

When the plate is warm, you are late

A warm plate with a heavy load on it can be normal-ish. A warm plate with a phone charger in it is a high-resistance joint. Kill power, pull the device, look at the screws. Blackened copper, melted insulation, a backstab that looks cooked — cut back to clean wire if you have slack, pigtail if you do not. If the insulation is baked into the cable jacket inside the box, you are replacing cable, not shopping for a prettier plate.

A wall checklist

  1. Prove the breaker is off at the slots, not at the panel legend.
  2. Device flush to the finished wall. Extender if the box is buried.
  3. Side screws, clockwise hooks, no backstabs on anything you care about.
  4. Yoke screws snug. Do not strip the ears to hide a floating box.
  5. Plug should grip. Plate should be cool after an hour with a real load.

A receptacle is a contact under a screw. When it wiggles, the contact is next. Tighten the yoke or replace the device before the brown halo around the slot becomes the headline.
