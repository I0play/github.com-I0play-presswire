The ceiling stain under the air handler is not a mystery leak. The condensate line is a biology experiment.

Your air conditioner does not just cool air. It wrings water out of it. That water is supposed to run down a PVC drip line to a drain or a pump. When the line clogs with slime, the pan fills, the float switch (if you have one) trips the system off, or the pan overflows into the drywall. People call an HVAC tech for a “refrigerant leak” and pay for a gallon of water that never left the house.

If the unit short-cycles, the closet smells like a gym bag, or there is a brown map on the ceiling under an attic handler, look at the condensate path before you authorize a recharge.

Find the line before you pour anything

Follow the white or cream PVC off the drain pan. There is usually a tee or a cleanout with a cap near the handler. Outside you may see a stub dripping next to the condenser. A condensate pump sits in a reservoir with a float and a little discharge tube to a sink or standpipe. Know which system you have. Pouring bleach into a pump reservoir is not the same job as flushing a gravity line.

Kill power at the air-handler switch or breaker if you are going to open the cabinet. Wet pan plus live electric is a stupid way to learn about service disconnects.

Clear the trap and the run

The trap exists so the fan does not suck air backward through the drain and blow water into the closet. It also collects gunk. Pull the cleanout cap. If the line is PVC you can often push a stiff nylon drain brush or a wet/dry vac on the outside termination and yank the slime toward you. Shop-vac on the cleanout, other end open, is the fastest honest method. You want a wet slurp, not a polite hum.

If you cannot get a vac on it, a wet/dry with a rag seal around the hose works. Do not feed a spinning drain cable into a thin-wall PVC stub unless you like buying a new line inside a finished ceiling.

Flush, then treat

After the mechanical clear, pour a cup of distilled white vinegar into the cleanout and let it sit. Vinegar beats random tablet chemistry for a homeowner who does not want to guess at metal compatibility in the pan. Warm water after. Some techs use a splash of household bleach in a gravity line. If you do, dilute it, do not mix it with vinegar in the same pipe, and keep it off the coil fins and off your clothes. The point is to knock back the biofilm, not to pickle the aluminum.

Condensate tablets in the pan are a maintenance habit, not a clog repair. A blocked line with a tablet on top is still a blocked line. Clear first.

The float switch is the only adult in the room

A secondary drain pan under an attic handler should have a float switch that opens the control circuit when the pan rises. If you do not have one, that brown ceiling is the switch. Clip-on pan switches are cheap and they sit in the auxiliary pan, not in your imagination. Test it by lifting the float while the thermostat is calling. The outdoor unit or the whole air handler should stop. If nothing happens, the switch is bypassed or dead. Fix that before you congratulate yourself on a clean pipe.

Primary pan switches on the trap tee do the same job closer to the source. Same test. Do not jumper them “just for the weekend.” Weekends become October.

Pumps fail loud, then wet

A condensate pump should click and spit water on a regular cadence when the system is wringing hard. If the reservoir is full and the motor is silent, the pump is done or the check valve is stuck. Dump the reservoir into a bucket, pull the inlet, flush the tank, and listen for the impeller. A pump that runs dry and screams needs water in the bowl or a replacement. Discharge tubing that is kinked behind the furnace is a clog that looks like a dead pump.

Route the discharge where it cannot siphon back. A high loop and a check valve exist for a reason. Dumping into a dirty slop sink is fine. Dumping onto a finished floor is how you meet your insurance adjuster.

A bench checklist

  1. Find the cleanout, the outdoor stub, or the pump. Know which drain you have.
  2. Power down before you open a wet cabinet.
  3. Vac the line from one end. Brush only what you can see.
  4. Vinegar soak, then warm water. Tablets after it flows, not instead of clearing.
  5. Lift the float switch and confirm the system stops. Add a pan switch if the attic has none.

Cold air makes water. Water needs a pipe. A pipe full of slime is a leak with a schedule. Clean it on a Saturday in cooling season and the drywall can stay boring.
