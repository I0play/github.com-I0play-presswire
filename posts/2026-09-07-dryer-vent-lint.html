If the jeans need two cycles, the vent is packed. Clean it before the lint becomes a heater.

Lint is fuel. The dryer is a box that blows hot air through fuel toward a plastic flap on the siding. When the pipe clogs, the air stays in the drum, the thermostat works overtime, the clothes stay damp, and the back of the machine gets hotter than the manual ever promised. People treat the screen in the door as the whole job. That screen catches the polite lint. The rest is in the elbow behind the dryer and in the run through the wall.

This is not a seasonal mood. It is a fire you can schedule. Pull the machine and look. If you have not seen the pipe in a year, you do not know what is in it.

Kill power and give yourself room

Unplug the dryer. Gas unit: unplug it anyway so the drum and the igniter are dead, and know where the gas shutoff is before you start sliding 200 pounds of enamel. Pull the machine straight out on a folded towel if you are scared of the floor. Do not crank on the vent hose like it is a weed. Crushing a foil accordion is how you make a new restriction.

The cheap foil hose that came with the house is a lint trap shaped like a slinky. Replace it with rigid or semi-rigid metal. Smooth inside. Short as the room allows. Every extra elbow is a place lint parks. White vinyl flex is for a shop-vac, not a dryer.

Clean the whole path, not just the pretty screen

Pop the hose off the dryer outlet and the wall. Vacuum both stubs. A shop-vac and a nylon dryer-vent brush on a flexible rod will pull gray felt out of a 4-inch pipe until you are slightly nauseated. Keep feeding rod until the brush exits the hood outside or you hit a grim stop. If you hit a stop in a short run, you have a crushed pipe or a bird nest. Fix the pipe. Do not keep running heat into a blockage.

Outside, open the hood. The flap should swing free and actually close when the fan is off. A hood packed with lint and a warped flap is a damper that never damps and a path that never flows. Clean it. If the flap is broken, replace the hood. Painting over a jammed plastic lid is not maintenance.

While the machine is out, vacuum the slot under the lint screen and the cavity behind it. That slot hides a second beard of lint that never reaches the hose. On many units you can lift the top or pop the lower panel and see the blower housing. If the housing looks felted, you found the rest of last winter’s towels.

How you know it worked

Reassemble metal-to-metal with a clamp, not a prayer and a zip tie over crushed foil. Slide the dryer back with the pipe unkinked. Run a hot empty cycle and stand outside. You want a strong push of warm air and a flap that opens like it means it. Inside, the cabinet sides should not feel like a range. Clothes that used to need 70 minutes should drop. If they do not, the restriction is still there or the heating element is tired. One problem at a time. Airflow first.

A long run through a cold crawl with three elbows and a screen nailed over the hood will never dry like a four-foot straight pipe. If the house layout is the enemy, shorten the run or add a proper booster designed for dryers. A bathroom fan in the line is not a booster. It is a lint tomb.

Do not invent a new hazard while you fix the old one

No screws through the pipe wall into the airstream. The points catch lint and start the clog you just removed. Use clamps on the outside. Keep the pipe off the gas line and off the cord. Leave enough slack to pull the machine next year without tearing the connection. If you smell hot dust or scorch, stop the cycle and pull the plug. Heat plus packed lint is not a riddle.

A bench checklist

  1. Unplug. Pull the dryer. Look at the real hose, not the door screen.
  2. Swap foil slinky for rigid or semi-rigid metal. Short, smooth, few elbows.
  3. Brush and vacuum the whole run until the outdoor hood breathes.
  4. Clamp joints. No screws in the airstream. Flap must swing.
  5. Test with a hot empty cycle. Strong exhaust outside, cooler cabinet, shorter dry time.

The screen in the door is a courtesy. The pipe is the job. Do the job before the next load of towels writes the ending.
