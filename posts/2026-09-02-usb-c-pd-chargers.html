Your cheap USB-C brick is lying to you. Here is how PD actually works.

USB-C looks simple because the connector is one shape. The power side is not simple. A laptop that wants 20 V, a phone that wants PPS, and a $9 Amazon brick that only ever wanted to dump 5 V into whatever you plugged in are all using the same hole in the wall. The difference is whether anything on the CC pin is telling the truth.

If you build gadgets, repair phones, or just keep buying chargers that get hot and die, you need the handshake, not the marketing sticker.

The connector is not the protocol

USB Type-C is the mechanical and electrical pinout. USB Power Delivery is a separate conversation that rides on the CC (Configuration Channel) wire. Data lines can be dead. You can have a perfectly valid high-power sink that never speaks USB data. That is why a power-only trigger board can pull 20 V out of a charger with no computer attached.

On attach, the source starts at a safe baseline: about 5 V on VBUS. Then it advertises a menu. That menu is a list of Power Data Objects (PDOs). A typical 65 W laptop brick says something like 5 V at 3 A, 9 V at 3 A, 15 V at 3 A, and 20 V at 3.25 A.

Your device picks one, the charger Accepts, ramps VBUS, then sends PS_RDY when the rail is actually there. Until PS_RDY, you do not have the contract. Cheap hardware skips steps, lies about current, or never implements the cable e-marker check that is required before going past 3 A.

Fixed PDOs vs PPS vs the high-voltage stuff

Fixed PDOs are the old reliable stairs: 5, 9, 15, 20 V in the Standard Power Range (up to 100 W). Easy for a buck converter. Ugly for a lithium cell that lives at 3.7–4.4 V, because the phone has to slam that 9 V down and turn the difference into heat in your pocket.

PPS (Programmable Power Supply) is the feature phones actually want. The sink can request a voltage in 20 mV steps inside an advertised window, plus a current limit. Charge circuitry can sit closer to the cell voltage, run cooler, and finish faster. If a box says Super Fast Charging and your phone still cooks, the brick probably advertised fixed 9 V and called it a day.

EPR (Extended Power Range) is the 28 / 36 / 48 V world that takes you toward 240 W. Different message types, stricter cables, and not something you casually feed into a hobby buck module rated for 24 V.

The cable is part of the circuit

Above 3 A the source is supposed to ask the cable who it is. An e-marked 5 A cable answers. A no-name cable with a printed 100W logo and no chip should force the source to stay conservative. Voltage drop on skinny copper is not academic. At 5 A, a few hundred milliohms becomes heat in the jacket and brownout at the laptop. If a cable is too hot to hold, throw it out.

How to test a charger

  1. Read the label. Count the PDOs. If it only lists 5 V / 9 V and claims 65 W, the math is already a lie.
  2. Use a USB-C power meter that understands PD. Plug charger to meter to known-good cable to device or load.
  3. Watch the contract, not just the watts. You want the voltage the device requested, not a sagging 4.6 V while the screen claims 20 W.
  4. Load it. A charger that holds 20.0 V at 100 mA and collapses to 17 V at 2 A is junk. So is one that smells like burnt flux after ten minutes.
  5. Unplug and replug in both orientations. Flaky one-way attach is a broken receptacle or a bent pin.

If you design boards: start at 5 V, confirm both orientations, then request a conservative PDO before you ask for 20 V. Instrument VBUS during plug, unplug, and load steps.

Trigger boards and the ways people get hurt

PD trigger modules that request a fixed 12 V or 20 V are useful on the bench. They are also a great way to feed 20 V into a 5 V ESP32 because you grabbed the wrong adapter. Label the output. Fuse the output. Assume you will plug it in half-asleep.

If you build a sink, implement the state machine or use a real controller. Do not just short CC with a resistor and pray. That old Rp/Rd trick only gets you Type-C default current at 5 V. It is not PD. It will not give you 20 V.

What to buy, what to bin

Keep one known-good brick and one known-good e-marked cable as your reference. Bin anything with no safety marks, a hollow-toy weight, a 100W print on shoelace copper, a wiggling receptacle, or heat you can feel through the desk after charging a phone.

USB-C PD is a negotiated contract on CC, not a promise printed next to a lightning bolt. Test with a meter that speaks PD, load the thing, and throw out anything that sags, stinks, or lies about current.
