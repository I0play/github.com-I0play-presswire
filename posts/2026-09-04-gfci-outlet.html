Swap that two-slot bathroom receptacle for a GFCI without turning the box into a bird’s nest or a fire.

A ground-fault circuit interrupter watches hot and neutral. If they differ by about 5 mA, it opens. That is the difference between a hair dryer in a sink and a trip to the ER. A regular duplex outlet in a wet room is not “fine because it has worked since 1994.” It has been lucky.

This is a like-for-like swap in an existing box: one cable in, maybe a downstream feed, a GFCI device that actually tests. It is not a new circuit from the panel. If the box is metal and crumbling, if you find two hots of different circuits in one yoke, if the insulation is cloth and crumbles in your hand — stop and call someone licensed. Pride is a bad insulator.

Kill the right breaker

Do not trust the marker on the panel door. Plug a cheap lamp or a receptacle tester into the outlet. Flip breakers until the lamp dies. Then check neighboring outlets and lights. Bathroom fans and lights are often on the same circuit. Confirm the wires in the box are dead with a non-contact tester and then with a meter hot-to-neutral and hot-to-ground. Non-contact testers lie near other cables. Meters lie less.

If you cannot find a breaker that kills it, you do not start unscrewing things. You find the breaker.

LINE is the feed. LOAD is a weapon if you swap them.

A GFCI receptacle has LINE screws (power in from the panel) and LOAD screws (protected power out to other outlets). If you land the feed on LOAD, the device will not reset, or it will sit there looking installed and do nothing useful. The extra set of screws is not “for neatness.”

Open the old outlet. Photograph it. One cable is usually the feed: hot on brass, neutral on silver, ground on green. If a second cable leaves the box toward the vanity or a garage outlet, that is a candidate for LOAD — only if you want those receptacles protected by this GFCI. If you do not know where the second cable goes, land only the feed on LINE, cap the extra hot and neutral separately, and test. You can move them to LOAD after you know what they feed.

Pigtail grounds. Do not stack three grounds under one green screw and hope. A short pigtail to the yoke, other grounds nutted in the back.

The box has to fit the brick

GFCI bodies are fat. A shallow old-work box stuffed with 12 AWG and a wad of tape will not close without crushing insulation. If the device sits proud, the plate will crack and the yoke will flex. Get a deeper box or an extension ring. Do not saw the plaster to make the plate lie. That is how you get a gap that collects toothpaste water.

Strip only enough insulation for the terminal. Hook the wire clockwise around a screw so tightening pulls it in. Backstab holes on a $9 GFCI are how you get a loose hot in six months. Use the screws. Torque them like the sheet says, not like you are hanging a porch swing.

Test it like you mean it

  1. Power back on. The GFCI may need a RESET press. If it will not latch, LINE and LOAD are swapped or a downstream ground-neutral bootleg is leaking.
  2. Press TEST. It must trip. RESET. It must stay on.
  3. Plug in a receptacle tester. You want correct wiring, not “open ground” and not “reversed.”
  4. If you used LOAD, plug the tester into the downstream outlet and hit TEST on the GFCI. That downstream receptacle should die too. If it stays live, you did not put that cable on LOAD, or you found a different circuit.

A tester that says “correct” on a two-wire ungrounded box with a GFCI is a special case: GFCI protection without an equipment ground is allowed in some replacements, with the “No Equipment Ground” sticker that came in the box. Do not invent a ground by stealing a water pipe and a prayer. Do not bootleg ground to neutral inside the box. That is how a GFCI sees a “fine” circuit while a chassis is sitting on neutral.

What “protected” actually means

A GFCI does not replace a breaker. It does not fix undersized wire. It does not make a bathroom heater safe on a 16 A continuous load. It trips on imbalance. AFCI is a different device for arcing in damaged cords. Combination dual-function breakers live in the panel. Do not confuse the three and then argue with an inspector.

Press TEST every month. Devices fail. Dust and age weld the mechanism. A GFCI that never trips when you press the button is a regular outlet with extra plastic.

When to walk away

Aluminum branch wire, a shared neutral (multi-wire branch circuit) on the LOAD terminals of a single-pole GFCI, a box with no ground and a metal plate that sits against a pipe, knob-and-tube in the wall — those are not Saturday projects. Cap it, put the old device back if you have to, and get a proper circuit. A working GFCI on a clean 15 A or 20 A grounded run is a one-hour job. Everything else is how bathrooms get famous.
