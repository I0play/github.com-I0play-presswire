The door does not stick because wood is moody. The strike is high and the hinge screws are spinning.

You hip-check the bedroom door and it finally clicks. In January it scrapes the head jamb. In July it will not latch unless you lift the knob. That is not a haunted house. The door dropped a few millimeters because the short screws in the top hinge pulled out of the jamb, and the strike plate is still where the latch used to be.

Paint and humidity change gaps a little. A hinge that is no longer fastened to framing changes them a lot. Fix the hinges into wood. Then move the strike a hair. Planing the door because you do not own a 3-inch screw is how you get a door that is too small in winter and still rubs in summer.

Look at the gaps before you sand

Close the door until the latch just kisses the strike. Look at the reveal around the slab. Even reveal, latch hits the hole: hardware is fine, stop fussing. Tight at the top latch side and open at the bottom hinge side: the slab sagged. Tight along the head on the latch corner: the stop or the strike is proud, or the slab swelled and you actually do need a plane — after the hinges are solid.

Open the door and yank the knob up and down. If the slab rocks on the hinges, the screws are loose or the mortise is crushed. That play is your scrape. Tightening a 3/4-inch screw into the same chewed hole does nothing. You are threading dust.

The top hinge is the load path

Most interior doors hang on three hinges. The top one carries the cantilever. Factory screws are often 3/4 inch into the jamb only. They back out. Pull the short screws from the top hinge leaf on the jamb side. Replace at least two of them with #9 or #10 screws long enough to bite the jack stud behind the jamb. In common 4-1/2-inch jambs that is usually 2-1/2 to 3 inches. Snug them. Do not crank until the hinge leaf dishes. A bent leaf throws the slab out of plane and you just invented a new rub.

If the hole is stripped, pack it. A sliver of hardwood with glue, or a proper plug, then re-drill a pilot. Toothpicks packed in dry are a temporary lie. The hinge must sit flat in the mortise. Paint blobs under the leaf count as a shim you did not want. Score and peel them.

Hinge pins that have walked up get a tap back down. If the pin is bent, replace the pin or the hinge. A banana pin is a squeak and a sag.

Move the strike, do not bully the latch

With the slab hanging true, close it slowly. The latch should hit the center of the strike opening, not the plate lip. If it lands high or low by a couple millimeters, unscrew the strike and file the opening, or shift the plate. Many strikes have a little vertical slot. Use it. If you need more than a file’s worth, chisel the mortise so the plate sits flush again. A strike standing proud of the jamb is a rattle and a snag.

If the door closes but will not stay latched, the hole is shallow or the strike is too far toward the stop. The latch bolt needs a square hole deep enough to throw. Foam weatherstrip smashed behind the stop can push the slab so the latch only kisses the plate. That is a stop problem, not a new lockset.

Deadlatch tabs on entry locks need the strike lip in the right place or the latch never fully extends. Then a credit card and a breeze both open your front door. That is not clever. That is a misaligned plate.

Swell is real. Plane last.

Unfinished edges drink humidity. A slab that grew across the width will rub the latch jamb even with perfect hinges. Confirm hinges are tight and the slab is square in the opening. Then mark the rub with chalk, take the door down, and plane the high spot — usually a strip near the latch or a corner at the head. Seal the raw edge with paint or varnish the same day or you get to do this again after the next rain week.

Do not plane the hinge edge to fix sag. You just made the mortises shallow and the next screw head stands up. Do not throw a folding shim behind one hinge leaf and walk away. That tilts the slab and moves the rub to a new corner.

The frame can walk too

Old houses settle. If the latch jamb leans in at the top, long screws through the jamb into the stud can pull it back a little. Go gentle. You can split a dry jamb. A case where the whole opening is a parallelogram is a frame job, not a strike job. You will feel it: even with the slab off, the opening is not rectangular. That is a different afternoon.

Interior hollow-core slabs are light. They still sag if the top hinge is jewelry. Solid-core and fire doors are heavier. Those get three serious screws into framing on the top hinge without a debate.

A bench checklist

  1. Read the reveal. Sag looks like a tight latch-side head and a wide hinge-side sill.
  2. Replace short top-hinge screws with screws that bite the stud. Seat the leaf flat.
  3. Close slowly and see where the latch hits the strike. File or move the plate. Keep it flush.
  4. Plane and seal only after the hardware is honest.
  5. If the opening itself is out of square, stop pretending a 2-inch file will fix the house.

A door that latches with two fingers is aligned hardware. A door that needs a hip is a hinge screw in dust and a strike in last year’s position. Put the steel into wood. Then the weather can do whatever it wants.
