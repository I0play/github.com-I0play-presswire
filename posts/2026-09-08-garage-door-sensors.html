The Bench

If the garage door reverses two inches off the slab, the photo-eyes are dirty, crooked, or both. Aim them before you buy a motor.

The door goes down, taps the concrete, and sulks back to the ceiling. You swear at the opener. The opener is doing what the safety loop told it to do. Across the opening sit two little sensors at ankle height. If they cannot see each other, the logic assumes a kid, a bumper, or a bicycle is in the way. It will not close. That is the feature. Treating it like a defect is how people wire the eyes out and wait for a lawsuit.

Most “broken opener” calls in a suburban garage are alignment, dust, sun, or a wire staple that bit the jacket. The motor is fine. The springs are fine. The beam is not.

What those two boxes are doing

One side emits infrared. The other side receives it. A solid LED on the receiver means the beam is locked. A blinking LED means it is hunting. No LED means no power or a dead eye. They mount on the tracks, roughly six inches off the floor, facing each other across a clear line. Kick a snow shovel in front of them and the door should refuse to close. If it closes anyway, the safety circuit is defeated or the eyes are not in the circuit. Stop and fix that before you tune anything else.

Clean, then aim

Wipe the lenses. Shop dust, cobwebs, and a film of rubber off the tires will kill a beam that looked fine last fall. Do not sand the plastic. A rag and a bit of glass cleaner is enough. Then look down the barrel. The two eyes should stare at each other, not at the opposite track at a cute angle because the bracket bent when you backed in a rake.

Loosen the wing nut. Hold the receiver until its LED goes solid. Snug. Repeat on the emitter if that side has a lamp. If the LED flickers when you tap the track with a knuckle, the bracket is loose or the track itself is dancing. Tighten the track to the wall before you chase photons.

Sunlight in the late afternoon can swamp a cheap receiver. A little hood — even a scrap of tape as a visor — keeps direct sun off the lens. Moving the receiver to the shadier jamb is cleaner than arguing with the sky.

The wire is part of the sensor

Two conductors, often white and white-with-black, back to the opener head. Staples that pinch the jacket short the pair when the door slams. You get intermittent reverse, usually on humid days or when the panel flexes. Run your fingers along the loom. Look for a staple that bit copper. Replace the damaged section. Do not twist-on a repair and leave the copper naked against a painted track.

Polarity matters on some brands. Swap the two leads at one eye and the LED stays dead. Swap them back. At the head, the terminals are labeled. If you pulled both pairs to paint the ceiling, you get to ring them out with a meter instead of guessing.

Force settings are not a substitute

The opener also has a close-force adjustment and a travel limit. If the door hits the floor and immediately reverses with the eyes solid and unobstructed, the close limit is too far or the force is too light on a stiff bottom seal. Nudge the close limit so the seal kisses, not so the motor stalls into concrete. Do not crank force to smash through a misaligned beam. That defeats the point of having a beam.

Test with a block of wood in the path. Door should stop and reverse. Test the beam by waving a broom through it while closing. Same result. If it does not reverse, the safety path is wrong. Unplug the opener and do not use the door on the wall button until that is true again.

When the eye is actually dead

LED never lights with known-good 12-ish volts at the head: replace that eye as a pair if the maker sells them that way. Cracked housings and water inside are end of life. Aftermarket generic eyes exist. Match the connector and the logic (some want a specific pulse). Cheap no-name pairs that “almost fit” will blink forever and train you to ignore the lamp.

A bench checklist

  1. Confirm both LEDs. Solid lock, not a blink you talk yourself into.
  2. Clean lenses. Aim until the lamp stays on when you tap the track.
  3. Inspect the two-wire run for staples that bit copper.
  4. Block the beam on a close cycle. It must reverse. If it does not, stop using the door.
  5. Only then touch close-limit and force. Never raise force to hide a dead eye.

The motor is the expensive part. The eyes are a ten-minute alignment and a rag. Do the rag first.
