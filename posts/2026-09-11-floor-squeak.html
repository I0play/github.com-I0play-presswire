The Bench

Sep 11, 2026 · Home DIY · ~8 minute read

The floor is not haunted. A nail backed out and the subfloor is sawing on a joist every time you walk to the kitchen.

Wood dries. Nails loosen. Two faces that used to sit still now slide a few thousandths and sing. A rug is camouflage. Powdered soap in the crack is folklore. The fix is to stop the motion: screw the subfloor to the joist, or sister the finish floor to the layer under it, from the side you can reach without turning the hallway into a jobsite for a week.

Find the exact board with your heel. Mark it. Guessing from the living room sofa is how you sink a screw into a plumbing run.

Top side when you cannot get under

If there is finish hardwood and no basement, you work from above with trim-head screws made for floors. They are thin, they snap off or sink below the face, and you putty the hole. Pilot if the species is hard. Drive into the joist, not into empty bay. A stud finder is mediocre through oak. A tap along the run plus the nail lines from the original install tell you where the lumber is.

Hit the squeak while someone walks it. One screw that misses the joist is a decoration. Two that catch the joist and pull the cup shut are the repair. Do not crank until the head buries an eighth inch and blows a chip. Snug is enough. You are clamping, not winching the house together.

From below is cleaner

Open basement or crawl: look up. A gap between subfloor and joist, a shiny nail that has worked, a block that never got nailed on both faces. Drive 2-inch construction screws up through the joist into the subfloor. Stay short of the finish floor thickness. Measure twice. A screw point in a 3/4-inch oak board is a souvenir you will feel forever.

Shims are for a consistent gap, not a moving one. A dry shingle tapped in and then screwed will hold. A loose shim that is not fastened becomes a rattle with extra steps. Construction adhesive on the joist-to-subfloor line helps the next season. It does not replace fasteners. Glue without screws is a wish.

What you are not doing

You are not sistering a 12-foot joist because one bay ticks. You are not tearing up good oak to replace a subfloor sheet that only talks at one nail. You are not shooting random 3-inch gold screws through a floating laminate. Floating floors squeak when the underlayment crumpled or the click joint is broken. Fastening a floating floor to the subfloor voids the point of a floating floor. Fix the lock or the lump. Do not nail it down and call it engineered.

Carpet over squeaky plywood is the easy version. Pull the corner if you must see, then screw through the carpet with trim screws and a piece of cardboard as a washer so the head does not drag yarn. Or work from below and leave the carpet alone.

Stairs are the same physics

A tread that yelps is usually the riser or the stringer let go. From below, a screw through the stringer into the tread and a bead of adhesive. From above, a finish screw at the back of the tread where the nosing shadow hides the hole. Do not slather oil soap on the squeak and declare tradition. Oil soap does not clamp wood.

How you prove it

Walk it the same path, same speed, same socks. If it still talks, you missed the joist or there is a second face rubbing two feet over. Mark again. One remaining chirp after three good screws is often a shoe mold or a heat register rubbing the cutout. That is a different two-minute trim job, not a framing crisis.

A bench checklist

  1. Pin the exact board with your weight. Mark it before you drill.
  2. Find the joist. Screw into lumber, not into a bay.
  3. From below if you can. Short enough that the point never kisses the finish floor.
  4. Adhesive is a helper. Fasteners do the work.
  5. Walk it again. If it still sings, look at the next joint instead of driving a dozen more decorative holes.

Squeaks are friction plus travel. Kill the travel. The house will still settle. The hallway does not have to announce every trip to the fridge.
