Under-cabinet LEDs that do not look like a frat house: aluminum channel, 24V, and a real dimmer

The peel-and-stick strip you slapped under the cabinets three years ago is now a dotted line of dead pixels and yellow adhesive. That is not bad luck. That is what happens when you mount a flexible PCB on painted wood, cook it with a 12 V supply running at 95% load, and never give the diodes a heat path.

Do it once. Use aluminum channel, 24 V tape, a driver with headroom, and a dimmer that matches the driver.

Why the cheap strip fails

LED tape is a row of diodes on a thin copper pour. The adhesive is not a mount. Heat has nowhere to go except into the cabinet bottom, which is an insulator. High-CRI tape at 10–14 W/m on 12 V also means more current for the same wattage, more drop along the run, and color shift by the sink.

Aluminum channel is the heat sink, the straight edge, and the frosted line that hides the dots. Skip it and you are installing a temporary light.

12 V vs 24 V

Same light output is available in both. 24 V halves the current for a given wattage. Voltage drop along a 2–3 m run becomes a non-event instead of a visible dim at the far end. Match the strip voltage to the driver. A 12 V strip on a 24 V driver dies fast. A 24 V strip on 12 V looks sad. That is the number-one unforced error in DIY lighting.

Parts that do not embarrass you

  • 24 V high-CRI tape (90+ CRI, 3000 K or 3500 K for kitchens)
  • Aluminum channel with a frosted cover — corner profile to wash the backsplash, surface profile to hit the counter
  • Mounting clips and end caps
  • A constant-voltage driver sized for load times 1.25. Five meters at 10 W/m is 50 W — buy 60 W or 75 W
  • A dimmer that speaks the same language as the driver: TRIAC on the AC side, or PWM / 0–10 V on the low-voltage side
  • 18 AWG for short low-voltage runs, 16 AWG if you cross the kitchen

Skip RGB party strips in a kitchen you cook in. One good warm-white scene and a dimmer beats a rainbow remote you will lose in a drawer.

Layout before you cut

Measure each cabinet run separately. Gaps over the stove and sink are separate channels, not one strip bent around a range hood. Park the driver above the fridge, in a pantry, or in a cabinet that does not see grease. Drivers hate heat and sealed boxes.

Decide whether the wall switch is line-voltage to the driver or low-voltage control. If you are not comfortable opening a box, hire an electrician for the AC side and do the low-voltage work yourself.

Cut, mount, feed

  1. Cut channel square. Deburr so the diffuser seats. End caps hide the cut.
  2. Clean the cabinet underside. Grease kills adhesive. Clips on 12–18 in centers. Pilot holes. Check cabinet thickness before you pick screw length.
  3. Cut tape only on the marked copper pads. Wrong cut means a dead segment or a short that takes the driver with it.
  4. Observe polarity. Mark the feed end.
  5. Solder or use proper clips, then insulate. Solder wins for anything trapped in a channel for a decade. Keep joints low-profile.
  6. Press the tape the full length of the channel. Air gaps become hot spots.
  7. Feed the lead out the end cap or a drilled hole. Do not pinch the jacket under the channel lip.

Wiring the driver

Low-voltage connections can live in a cabinet. Line-voltage connections belong in a box. Ground the metal if the chassis expects it. Strain-relief the cable. Do not bury a driver under cookbooks.

  • TRIAC dimmable driver plus a compatible LED dimmer on the hot feeding the driver
  • PWM controller on the 24 V output for low-voltage control, FET sized for actual current
  • 0–10 V if that is what your switch already is — run the pair properly

Power up on the bench first. Confirm every segment before you snap fifteen feet of diffuser in place and find a reversed jumper over the sink.

Finish work people skip

Aim the channel. A 45-degree wash hides the source. A straight down-fire is better for chopping onions. Cap every end. Label the driver with voltage, wattage, and date.

Done looks like this: no dots, no ice-cave blue, no brick hanging from an outlet strip. A continuous warm line, a switch that dims without strobing, and a driver that is barely warm after an hour at full output. That is a weekend, not a 20-minute short — which is why the 20-minute version fails in six months.
