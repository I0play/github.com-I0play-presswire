If the kitchen tap ticks after you shut it off, the cartridge is shot. Replace it before you buy a whole faucet.

Drip. Drip. A cup a night into the sink. You tighten the handle until it feels like a valve on a ship and it still weeps. That is not “the washer.” Most kitchen faucets sold this century use a ceramic-disc cartridge. The discs wear, the O-rings flatten, mineral scale parks itself in the seats. A $15–40 cartridge is the repair. A $200 faucet is the surrender.

Single-handle mixer: one cartridge under the handle. Two-handle: a cartridge or a stem in each side. Pull-down sprayers add a separate drip at the head that is usually a failed check valve or a hose, not the body cartridge. Diagnose before you order parts like a tourist.

Prove it is the cartridge

Shutoff under the sink. If you do not have stops, install them after this job because living without them is how you flood a cabinet. Open the faucet. If water still arrives, the stops are lies and you need the house valve.

A drip from the spout with the handle off is the cartridge or the seats. A drip from under the handle is a failed O-ring on the cartridge or a loose retaining nut. A drip only when the sprayer is docked is often the diverter or the head. Do not replace the body because the spray head gasket is $8.

Get the exact part

Brand and model are stamped under the spout, on the supply hoses, or on a tag you threw away in 2019. Photograph the old cartridge when it is out. Moen, Delta, Pfister, Kohler, and the big-box house brands do not share internals. “Universal cartridge” in a blister pack is a coin flip. Take the old one to the counter or match the stem shape and the locating tabs from the manufacturer diagram.

Buy the O-rings and the retainer clip if they sell a kit. Buy silicone grease made for potable water, not bearing grease from the garage.

Pull it without wrecking the valve body

Close the stops. Open the faucet to bleed pressure. Pop the index cap on the handle, remove the set screw — usually 3/32 or 2.5 mm hex — and lift the handle. Unscrew the dome or bonnet. Some brands hide a horseshoe clip. Pull that clip with pliers, do not pry the plastic housing until it splits.

Cartridges seize. A manufacturer puller is cheap compared with a scored valve body. Twist while pulling straight. If you wreck the retaining ears you are buying a faucet. Do not hammer. Do not heat the brass with a torch under a particle-board sink.

Look at the seats in the body. Grit and broken ceramic stay there and will murder the new discs in a week. Flush both supplies into a bucket with the cartridge out — brief, controlled, rag over the opening so you do not redecorate the cabinet. Clean the bore. A smear of plumber silicone on the new O-rings, not a fistful.

Clock the cartridge

Tabs and flats have a home. Hot on the left when you face the sink in a standard US install. If hot and cold are swapped after you finish, the cartridge is 180 degrees out or the supplies were crossed under the deck in 2004. Pull it and rotate before you invent a conspiracy.

Clip in. Bonnet snug, not gorilla. Handle on. Stops open. Check the spout and the handle boss. Cycle hot and cold a dozen times to seat the discs. A tiny weep that stops after a few cycles can be grease and air. A drip that continues is a missed O-ring, a clip not seated, or grit you left in the bore.

When the faucet is actually done

Cracked metal spout, seized diverter that will not take a part, and plastic bodies with stripped retainer threads are end of life. A cartridge will not fix a hose that bursts when you tug the sprayer. Look at the supply lines while you are under there. Those cheap encased lines split at the nut. Braided stainless with new gaskets is a thirty-minute upgrade.

Hard water eats cartridges. If this is the third one in four years, a phosphate filter or a real softener is cheaper than a hobby of dripping brass.

Do this, then wash the coffee mug

  1. Stops off. Handle off. Clip out. Cartridge straight out.
  2. Flush the body. Match the part number, not the aisle endcap.
  3. Grease O-rings. Seat the tabs. Clip fully home.
  4. Open stops, cycle, watch the spout and the under-handle joint.
  5. If it still drips, the seats or the body are the problem — not “the cheap cartridge they sold you.”

A kitchen drip is a ceramic part you can hold in your palm. Swap it on a Saturday and keep the faucet that already fits the hole in the deck.
