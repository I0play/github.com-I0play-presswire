The Bench

Sep 3, 2026 · Electronics · ~8 minute read

If the terminal slides off when you tug, you did not crimp it. You mashed copper with a gas-station pliers and called it wiring.

Solder on a stranded power lead looks tidy until vibration work-hardens the joint and the wire snaps at the edge of the blob. A real crimp is a cold weld: barrel metal flows into the strands, gas-tight, no flux, no wick, no fatigue notch. That only happens if the terminal matches the wire, the tool matches the terminal, and you stop using the $6 combo stripper that has a “crimp” hole stamped into the handle like a novelty.

This is the shop version. 12–22 AWG power, sensors, automotive, bench harnesses. Not jewelry. Not Ethernet. If the joint can carry amps and live in a drawer that gets slammed, treat it like a mechanical part.

The terminal is a spec, not a vibe

Insulated vinyl-sleeve terminals from a mixed bag are sized by wire range printed on the sleeve or the bag, not by how hard you squeeze. Red / blue / yellow in North America is roughly 22–18 / 16–14 / 12–10 AWG. That is a starting hint, not a religion. Cheap bags lie. Measure the conductor OD after you strip it and pick a barrel that swallows the strands without a cave-in or a hollow.

Uninsulated open-barrel terminals (the kind that look like two little wings) are what appliances and cars actually use. The wings fold over the conductor, a second set folds over the insulation as a strain relief. Closed-barrel insulated terminals are fine for a one-off ring lug on a PSU. Mixing them with the wrong die is how you get a shiny pancake that looks “crushed enough.”

If the insulation jacket is thick (silicone, extra-flex, high-strand “welding” wire), a standard barrel will look full and still have a void in the middle. Either strip longer and double the conductor back on itself for a closed barrel, or buy terminals rated for that jacket and strand count.

The tool is not optional

A ratcheting crimper with interchangeable dies pays for itself the first time a 12 V rail does not go high-resistance behind a cabinet. The ratchet should not release until the die has closed. If you can chicken-out halfway, the tool is a toy.

  • Insulated closed-barrel: a die that leaves a smooth, slightly oval barrel, not a cut through the vinyl.
  • Open-barrel / “F” crimp: a die that rolls the conductor wings in and leaves the insulation wings hugging the jacket. The conductor crimp should look like a “B” in cross-section, not a smashed “U.”
  • Ferrules: a square or trapezoid die sized to the ferrule, not a pair of sidecutters you squeezed “just a little.”

Do not solder after a good crimp “because it feels safer.” Solder wicks under the insulation, turns the flex section into a solid rod, and the wire breaks there. Solder is for PCB pads and splices you will heat-shrink and never bend again.

Strip length is the whole game

Strip so the conductor fills the barrel and a whisker of copper is visible at the tongue end on a closed barrel. Too short and you are crimping jacket. Too long and stray strands stab the next terminal in a dense block. On open-barrel, the conductor sits only in the conductor wings. Jacket sits only in the insulation wings. If copper shows under the insulation crimp, you stripped too far. If jacket is in the conductor crimp, you stripped too short. Both fail.

Use a decent stripper. Nicking strands is how a 16 AWG becomes 18 AWG with a stress riser. If you see shiny cut faces on half the strands, cut it off and strip again.

The pull test is not macho theater

After the crimp, tug like you mean it. The wire should break or the insulation should tear before the terminal lets go. If the conductor walks out, the die was oversized, the barrel was undersized for that strand count, or you released the ratchet early. Cut it off. Do not “tighten it a second time.” A second mash work-hardens the barrel and splits it.

On power connections, add the electrical test: milliohms if you have a meter that can do it, or at least a loaded voltage drop. A pretty crimp that drops 0.4 V at 8 A is a heater. Re-terminate.

A bench habit that stops mystery faults

  1. Cut, strip, twist only enough to keep strands together. Do not make a tight rope that refuses to fill the barrel.
  2. Seat the terminal in the correct die color or cavity. Look at it before you squeeze.
  3. Crimp once, full ratchet.
  4. Inspect: no cracked vinyl, no cut strands, insulation wings on jacket, conductor wings on copper.
  5. Pull test. Heat-shrink if the environment is wet or abrasive. Label the other end while you still remember which lead is which.

What to throw in a drawer and what to bin

Keep one ratcheting tool, a small set of dies you actually use, and terminals from a vendor that prints AWG and stud size on the bag. Bin the mixed “250 pcs auto kit” whose yellow terminals split on the first crimp. Bin pliers-style “crimpers” that leave a cut through the conductor. Bin anything you “fixed” with a dab of solder and electrical tape.

A harness that survives a year in a car door or on a shop cart is boring: same terminals, same die, same tug. Boring is the point. If it comes apart in your hand on the bench, it was never going to survive the job.
