The Bench

Sep 4, 2026 · Electronics · ~8 minute read

Hooking a 5 V module to a 3.3 V GPIO because “it worked on the breadboard” is how pins die on a Tuesday.

ESP32, RP2040, STM32, nRF52: most of the chips you actually use live at 3.3 V. Half the modules in the junk drawer are leftover Arduino 5 V boards — old OLED backpacks, cheap ultrasonic bricks, RS-232 adapters, “I2C” LCD packs with a PCF8574 and a backlight that drinks 80 mA. People wire SDA straight across and call it a day because the first scan printed an address.

That is not a design. That is luck with a clamp diode. The MCU pin has a tiny ESD diode to 3.3 V. A 5 V output shoves current through that diode into the rail. Sometimes the chip resets. Sometimes the pin leaks forever. Sometimes the 3.3 V regulator gets lifted and every other device on the rail starts acting drunk. You will not see it in a five-minute demo.

What “tolerant” actually means

Read the datasheet pin table, not a forum post. “5 V tolerant” means the pin can sit at 5 V while the chip is powered at 3.3 V, usually only as an input, and usually not when analog or USB-adjacent. It does not mean the pin can output 5 V. It does not mean you can power the chip at 0 V and feed 5 V into a GPIO. Unpowered-tolerant is a different sentence, and most parts fail that test.

ESP32 GPIO is not 5 V tolerant. RP2040 is not. Many STM32 pins are, on specific packages, with notes. If you have to hunt the footnote, treat it as 3.3 V and shift.

One direction is a resistor. Two directions is not.

A 5 V device talking at a MCU input: a series resistor of 2.2 kΩ to 10 kΩ plus the MCU clamp is a crude limiter. Better: a MOSFET or a dedicated unidirectional buffer. Best when the 5 V side is push-pull: a 74LVC1T45 or 74AHCT125 powered on the 5 V side if you need a 5 V output from a 3.3 V MCU.

I2C and one-wire are open-drain. They go both ways on the same wire. A pair of resistors will not shift that. The cheap “four-channel bidirectional module” with a BSS138 and two 10 kΩ resistors is the correct topology for I2C if you wire it honestly: LV to 3.3 V, HV to 5 V, grounds common, drain of the FET on each side of the bus. Do not power LV from 5 V because the silkscreen was small. Do not stack two of those boards and then add more pull-ups until the rise time looks like a ramp.

Pull-ups live on one voltage

An I2C bus wants pull-ups to the logic level of that segment. 3.3 V bus: pull to 3.3 V. 5 V bus: pull to 5 V. If both sides already have 4.7 kΩ to their own rails, and you slap a shifter in the middle, you now have two networks. That is fine if the FET is doing its job. It is not fine if you also soldered 2.2 kΩ “for speed” on both ends and the open-drain pin cannot sink the current. Devices disappear at 400 kHz and magically return at 50 kHz. That is rise time, not haunted silicon.

Scope SDA. If the edge never reaches 70 percent of the rail before the clock, slow the bus or raise the pull-up value on the long side, not both.

Parts that earn their keep

  • Unidirectional 3.3 → 5 V output: 74AHCT125 or 74HCT245 running on 5 V. HCT input threshold is happy with 3.3 V CMOS high.
  • Unidirectional 5 → 3.3 V input: 74LVC245 on 3.3 V, or a series resistor plus a 3.3 V clamp if you are cheap and the edge is slow.
  • Auto-direction, mixed: TXS0108 / TXB0108 only if you read the app note. TXB hates pull-ups and long cables. TXS wants pull-ups and still sulks past a meter of wire.
  • I2C only: PCA9306 or the BSS138 discrete pair. PCA9306 has an enable. Use it so an unpowered 5 V widget does not load the live 3.3 V bus.

Skip the “I just used a diode” trick unless you can explain where the high level comes from on the way back. A diode drops voltage in one direction and leaves the other side floating. That is not a shifter. That is a prayer.

Ground is not optional

Level shifters need a common ground. A module powered from a wall wart and an MCU on USB with no ground wire between them will “sometimes work” when both chassis leak through you. Run GND first, then data. If the 5 V supply can be unplugged while the 3.3 V side stays live, make sure the shifter and the 5 V device cannot back-feed the 3.3 V rail through protection diodes. Enable pins and series resistors exist for that afternoon.

A five-minute check before you commit a PCB

  1. List every net that leaves the MCU. Mark voltage and direction. Open-drain vs push-pull.
  2. If any 5 V output can touch a 3.3 V pin, put a real translator on that net.
  3. Do not share one TXB0108 across UART, SPI, and a button. Those are different jobs.
  4. Power the shifter from the rails it claims to speak. Measure both sides with a meter, not hope.
  5. Hot-plug the 5 V gadget once on the bench with current-limited supply. If 3.3 V jumps, you still have a sneak path.

Voltage is not a personality trait of a breakout board. It is a contract. Honor it with one FET or one 74-series gate, or replace the MCU when the pin map starts having holes.
