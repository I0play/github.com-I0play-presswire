A bathroom fan that rattles and does nothing is a mold machine. Swap it. Do not “clean it later.”

The grille is furry, the motor sounds like a blender full of gravel, and the mirror still fogs for twenty minutes after a shower. That is not ambiance. That is moisture sitting in drywall, paint, and the back side of the vanity. A fan that does not move air to the outdoors is a noise maker. Replace the unit, keep the hole if you can, and stop pretending a candle and an open door are ventilation.

This is a same-size swap in a finished ceiling. If you have no duct, a duct that dumps into the attic, or a fan wired with a lamp cord from 1987, you have a bigger job. Deal with that honestly before you buy a pretty grille.

Kill the circuit like you mean it

Find the breaker. Flip it. Confirm the fan and the bath light (if they share a switch) are dead with a non-contact tester and a meter on the wires once the grille is off. Bathroom ceilings sit over wet floors. You do not troubleshoot live splices from a step stool with wet socks.

If the fan and light are on one switch and you want them separate, that is a cable run and a double switch, not a “smarter fan.” Plan it or leave it combined.

Measure the hole, not the box art

Pull the grille. Most old units squeeze out of a sheet-metal housing that stays in the joists; some are one piece. Measure the housing length and width, and the joist bay if you can see it. Typical retrofit housings are in the 8 × 8 in or 9 × 10 in neighborhood. Buy a retrofit / remodel fan rated to fit that opening, or accept that you are cutting drywall and patching.

CFM is not a personality. A small bath wants on the order of 50–80 CFM if the duct is short and straight. A big bath or a long flex run wants more. Sone rating is the noise. Under 1.0 sone is quiet enough that people will actually use the switch. A 3-sone bargain fan gets left off, which is how you grew the mold in the first place.

Duct size matters more than the sticker. A 3 in duct on a 110 CFM fan is a joke. Match or upsize the duct. Rigid or semi-rigid is better than cheap accordion flex that kinks behind the fan and turns 80 CFM into a wheeze.

Where the air actually goes

If the old duct ends in the attic with a flap and a prayer, you are humidifying insulation. Run it to a roof cap or a wall cap with a backdraft damper, sloped so condensate does not run back into the motor. Insulate the duct in a cold attic or you get a rainstorm inside the pipe every winter morning.

Do not dump a bath fan into a kitchen range hood, a garage, or a soffit that also feeds the attic intake. You are just moving the problem.

The swap

  1. Grille off. Photograph the wiring. Note which conductor is hot, neutral, ground. Baths are supposed to be grounded. If you find two cloth wires and no ground, stop and decide if you are fishing a ground or calling this a larger electrical job.
  2. Disconnect in the fan’s junction box, not by cutting mid-cable like a raccoon.
  3. If the housing stays: remove the motor plate, usually two screws or squeeze clips. Slide the old blower out. Clean the housing. If the housing is rusted through, the housing comes out too.
  4. If the whole unit comes down: support it. Housing flanges are often nailed into joists from above. From below you may need to compress hanger bars or cut nails. Have a helper or a prop. A fan in the face is a stupid injury.
  5. Mount the new housing flush to the finished ceiling, not proud, not recessed in a crater. Hanger bars onto joists if you have access. Retrofit clips if you do not.
  6. Duct clamp plus foil tape made for ducts. Duct tape is a brand name and a lie in attics.
  7. Wire with wirenuts or lever nuts that actually bite. Ground the housing. Button the junction cover before you energize.
  8. Install the blower, grille, restore power, hold a scrap of tissue to the grille. If it does not stick, you have a duct or damper problem, not a “break-in period.”

Humidity switches and timers

A humidistat fan that runs after you leave is the only fan that works in a house full of people who will not wait. Put the sensor where steam actually is, not in a dead corner. A simple spring timer switch is the cheap version and still beats a single-pole that nobody remembers.

Do not put a bath fan on a GFCI that also feeds an outdoor receptacle if you can avoid the nuisance trips. Follow the local code for the circuit you are on. If you do not know the circuit, you are not done looking at the panel.

The attic mistakes that make a new fan useless

Crushed flex, a roof cap full of wasp nest, a damper glued shut with old paint, a housing buried in blown-in insulation so the motor cooks — all of these survive a “I replaced the fan” afternoon. Look from above if you can. Clear the cap. Keep insulation off the motor housing unless the unit is rated to be buried, which most cheap ones are not.

Patch the ceiling only after the new grille covers the hole. If you over-cut, a larger grille or a thin extra ring of drywall plus compound is cleaner than hoping the plastic lid floats over a 2-inch gap.

A quiet fan, a real duct to weather, and a switch people will use. That is the whole repair. Everything else is a grille that looks new while the paint peels behind the toilet.
